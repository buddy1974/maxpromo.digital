## The Scenario

The domain is registered in the developer's name. The hosting account at GoDaddy or Hostinger is on their email address. You paid for the website — but legally and technically, control sits elsewhere.

We've seen this repeatedly over the years. Not always with bad intent — often it was just set up that way because the developer "handled everything" and nobody asked who owned what.

## What to Do Immediately

**Step 1: Research the current state.**

- WHOIS lookup for your domain: who is listed as the registrant? (whois.domaintools.com or directly with the registrar)
- Identify the hosting provider: do you know where the site is hosted? Netcraft and similar tools can show this.
- Do you have any credentials at all? Emails with login details, old invoices from the hosting provider?

**Step 2: Establish legal clarity.**

The domain belongs to you — demonstrable through purchase records, email correspondence, and commission documents. Most registrars (Hostinger, GoDaddy, IONOS) have processes for exactly this situation. It requires documentation, but it's resolvable.

**Step 3: Contact the hosting provider.**

Hosting providers won't simply transfer an account — but they have support processes for disputes. A formal request with evidence of your commission and payment is the right approach.

## What Helps

- All emails with the developer (commission, sign-off, payments)
- Invoices or bank transfer records for the original project
- Evidence that you paid for hosting and domain (if the developer billed these through to you)

## What Happens Next

Once access is restored: immediately transfer all credentials to your own accounts. Create new admin credentials. Remove the old developer's access.

This is also the right moment to think about the platform: a modern system with clear ownership and self-service maintenance prevents this situation from repeating itself.
